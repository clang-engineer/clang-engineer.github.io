# A Guide to Eunyul Talchum

This guide is intended for readers who are unfamiliar with Korean mask dance drama. It provides the cultural and performance context needed before reading the English translation of the Eunyul Talchum performance script.

Korean terms that do not have an exact English equivalent are generally retained in romanized form. A short English description is provided to help readers understand the term without replacing its Korean cultural meaning.

## 1. About Eunyul Talchum

**Eunyul Talchum (은율탈춤)** is a Korean mask dance drama associated with Eunyul in Hwanghae Province. Its performance combines masked characters, dance, dialogue, song, instrumental music, satire, and ritual elements.

The surviving performance is organized into six **gwajang (과장)**, or performance scenes:

1. Lion Dance
2. Heotmok (Sangjwa) Dance
3. Eight Mokjung Dance
4. Yangban Dance
5. No-seung Dance
6. Yeonggam and Halmi Dance

The performance traditionally begins with a procession (*gilnori*) and opens with a large white lion dance. In Eunyul Talchum, this opening lion dance has a **byeoksa (벽사)** function: it ritually drives away harmful influences and purifies the performance ground.

## 2. Characters

The English descriptions below are explanatory glosses, not replacements for the Korean character names. In the script, Korean names are retained wherever they carry a culturally specific identity or dramatic function.

### Sangjwa (상좌) — Young Buddhist Acolyte

A youthful Buddhist figure appearing in the second scene, also called **Heotmok (헛목)** in Eunyul Talchum. The character wears white clothing and performs a largely dance-centered scene.

### Mokjung (목중) — Monk Character

One of eight masked monk characters appearing in the third scene. The Eight Mokjung scene combines individual entrances, dance, calls to the musicians, and comic dialogue. The term is retained as *Mokjung* rather than reduced simply to “monk,” since it denotes a specific stock character of Korean mask dance drama.

### Yangban (양반) — Member of the Traditional Elite

A character representing the *yangban*, the elite social class of premodern Korea. Eunyul Talchum features three Yangban brothers. Their scene forms part of the mask drama’s satire of social hierarchy.

### Malttugi (말뚝이) — Servant Character

A servant associated with the Yangban scene. Although outwardly serving the Yangban, Malttugi uses comic speech and verbal maneuvering to mock and undermine their authority.

### No-seung (노승) — Old Buddhist Monk

An old Buddhist monk character appearing in the fifth scene. His costume and props visibly identify him with Buddhist monastic life, while the drama places him within a comic and worldly situation.

### Saemaeksi (새맥시) — Young Woman Character

A young female character who appears in the performance, notably in connection with the No-seung scene. The Korean character name is retained because a simple translation such as “young woman” does not fully represent her role as a named mask-drama type.

### Choegwari (최괄이)

A distinctive male character of Eunyul Talchum. The name is retained in romanization. His mask is especially striking, with multiple protruding lumps, and he appears in the later comic action of the performance.

### Yeonggam (영감) — Elderly Husband

An older male character in the final scene and the husband of Halmi. Here *Yeonggam* is a character designation rather than a generic English “old man,” so the Korean term is retained.

### Halmi / Miyal Halmi (할미 / 미얄할미) — Old Woman

The elderly wife of Yeonggam in the final scene. Conflict between the elderly couple and a younger woman develops into the final dramatic action. The character is also associated with the ritual conclusion of the performance.

### Mudang (무당) — Korean Shaman

A ritual specialist in Korean shamanic tradition. A *mudang* appears in the final part of Eunyul Talchum, where ritual performance follows the dramatic action. The term is retained because “shaman” alone does not convey its specifically Korean cultural context.

### Saja (사자) — Lion

The lion of the opening scene is a large white performance figure operated by three performers. Unlike lion scenes in some other Korean mask dramas, the Eunyul lion appears at the beginning of the performance and functions as a ritual purifier of the performance ground.

### Mabu (마부) — Handler / Groom

A character associated with the lion in the opening procession and performance context. The exact English rendering may vary with the dramatic context; *Mabu* is therefore retained when used as a character designation.

### Jaebi (잽이) — Musician

A musician who accompanies the mask dance. The *jaebi* are not merely background accompanists: performers may directly call to or exchange words with them as part of the performance.

## 3. Masks, Costumes, and Props

### Tal (탈) — Mask

A mask worn by a performer in Korean mask dance drama. Eunyul Talchum uses visually differentiated masks to establish character identity. Colors, facial features, wrinkles, lumps, and other exaggerated features contribute to characterization.

### Hansam (한삼) — Long Sleeve Extensions

Long cloth extensions attached at the ends of the sleeves. They enlarge the visual line of the performer’s arm movements and are actively used in dance gestures, particularly in the Mokjung dances.

### Gokkal (고깔) — Conical Ceremonial Hat

A pointed cloth hat associated with Buddhist and traditional ceremonial contexts. Sangjwa and the Mokjung characters wear *gokkal* as part of their stage costume.

### Jangsam (장삼) — Buddhist Robe

A long robe associated with Buddhist clothing. Sangjwa wears a white *jangsam* in Eunyul Talchum.

### Gasa (가사) — Buddhist Ceremonial Vestment

A Buddhist vestment worn over the robe. In the Sangjwa costume, a floral *gasa* is worn over the shoulders.

### Songnak (송낙) — Monk’s Hat

A traditional hat associated with Buddhist monks. No-seung wears a *songnak* together with Buddhist robes.

### Baekpal Yeomju (백팔염주) — 108-Bead Buddhist Rosary

A Buddhist rosary of 108 beads. It is worn by No-seung and helps identify the character visually as a Buddhist monk.

### Moktak (목탁) — Wooden Buddhist Percussion Instrument

A wooden percussion instrument used in Buddhist practice. No-seung carries or wears a *moktak* as part of his monastic characterization.

## 4. Music and Dance

### Jangdan (장단) — Rhythmic Cycle

A recurring rhythmic framework in Korean traditional music. In Eunyul Talchum, different *jangdan* organize both musical accompaniment and dance movement. The term is retained because it refers to more than a simple Western-style “beat.”

Among the rhythmic patterns used in Eunyul Talchum are **Taryeong jangdan (타령장단)**, **Gutgeori jangdan (굿거리장단)**, **Domburi jangdan (돔부리장단)**, and **Dol jangdan (돌장단)**.

### Bullim (불림) — Performer’s Call to the Musicians

A vocal call through which a performer asks the musicians for a rhythmic accompaniment. The speed or character of the *bullim* can help determine the tempo of the following *jangdan*. Because it is part of the interaction between performer and musicians, it should not be treated merely as dialogue.

### Chumsawi (춤사위) — Dance Movement / Dance Phrase

A named or recognizable unit of movement within Korean traditional dance. In the script, descriptions of *chumsawi* are treated as performance directions rather than ordinary narrative prose.

## 5. Ritual and Cultural Terms

### Byeoksa (벽사) — Ritual Expulsion of Harmful Influences

A ritual concept referring to driving away harmful or inauspicious forces. The opening lion dance of Eunyul Talchum has a *byeoksa* function: the white lion dances before the dramatic scenes to purify the performance ground.

### Gut (굿) — Korean Shamanic Ritual

A ritual performed within Korean shamanic tradition, typically led by a *mudang*. When the term occurs in the performance context, *gut* is retained rather than translated simply as “ritual,” since it refers to a culturally specific form of ritual performance.

### Gilnori (길놀이) — Opening Procession

A procession held before the main mask-dance performance. Performers, characters, and musicians move together through the local area before entering the performance ground.



## 7. Additional Ritual, Costume, and Performance Terms

### Gosa (고사) — Ritual Offering

A ritual offering performed to seek protection, well-being, or favorable conditions before an undertaking. The source records that, before Eunyul Talchum was performed, all members participated in a *gosa* addressed to **Cheonji Sinmyeong**.

### Cheonji Sinmyeong (천지신명) — Divine Powers of Heaven and Earth

A collective expression for sacred or spiritual powers associated with heaven and earth. Because the phrase does not designate a single deity, it is retained in Korean and glossed rather than translated as a singular “God.”

### Gongsu (공수) — Spirit Message

A message delivered through a *mudang* while speaking on behalf of a spirit during a shamanic ritual. In the final scene, the *mudang* conveys Halmi's words to Yeonggam through *gongsu*.

### Yukhwanjang (육환장) — Six-Ringed Buddhist Staff

A Buddhist staff fitted with six metal rings. It forms part of No-seung's visible monastic equipment.

### Aek-un (액운) — Harmful or Inauspicious Fortune

Misfortune or an inauspicious influence understood as something that may be ritually removed or sent away. The source explains that burning the masks after the performance was associated with sending away the *aek-un* of performers and spectators.

### Deogeori (더거리) — Three-Paneled Outer Garment

A traditional outer garment described in the source as having three hanging panels and historically worn by several categories of military or official personnel during the Joseon period. The term is retained because there is no precise modern English garment equivalent.

### Baerye (배례) — Formal Bowing / Paying Respects

A formal act of bowing or paying respects. In the Heotmok (Sangjwa) scene, the performer makes *baerye* toward the four directions before beginning the dance.

### Taeguk-won (태극원) — Taegeuk Circle Formation

A circular ensemble formation made by the Eight Mokjung as they dance out of the performance ground. The Korean name is retained because it refers to a specific choreographic formation.

### Hallyang (한량) — Cultivated Man of Leisure / Merrymaker

A historically layered social and cultural term. In the Second Mokjung's speech, it describes someone who knows how to enjoy and participate properly in the performance world, in contrast to a mere passing guest. The term is therefore retained in the script rather than reduced to a single English social label.

## 8. Note on Classical Quotations in Bullim

Many *bullim* lines are compact quotations or adaptations of Classical Chinese poetry, celebratory couplets, or proverbial expressions. Where the source annotations identify the original sense, the English translation follows that documented meaning. Where the transmitted performance wording is corrupt or differs across recorded versions, the variant is noted rather than silently regularized.

## 9. Reading the Script

Each scene file begins with a short list of the characters appearing in that scene. These are concise reading aids; fuller descriptions of culturally specific characters and terms are collected in this guide.

Stage directions are distinguished from spoken dialogue. Korean performance terms are retained where an English substitution would erase an important cultural or theatrical distinction.

This guide will be expanded as the translation proceeds and additional terms requiring cultural explanation appear in the script.
