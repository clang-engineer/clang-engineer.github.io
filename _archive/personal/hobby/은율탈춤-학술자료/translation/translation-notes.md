# Eunyul Talchum English Translation Notes

This document records the working principles used for the English translation of the Eunyul Talchum performance script. It is intended to keep terminology, character names, stage directions, and culturally specific concepts consistent across scene files.

## 1. Translation Goal

The translation should function as an academically responsible but readable performance script for readers who may have no prior knowledge of Korean mask dance drama.

The priority is not word-for-word substitution. The translation should:

- preserve culturally specific Korean concepts where an English equivalent would be misleading;
- remain understandable to an international reader;
- preserve the oral, comic, rhythmic, and performative qualities of the dialogue where possible;
- distinguish dialogue, song, calls, musical cues, and stage directions;
- avoid silently replacing Korean religious, social, or theatrical concepts with superficially similar Western ones.

## 2. Romanization and Korean Terms

Culturally specific terms are generally retained in romanized Korean when translation alone would erase an important distinction.

On first occurrence where helpful:

**Sangjwa (상좌) — young Buddhist acolyte**

Subsequent occurrences may use **Sangjwa** alone.

Character names used as dramatic roles are retained in romanization, for example:

- Sangjwa
- Mokjung
- Malttugi
- No-seung
- Saemaeksi
- Choegwari
- Yeonggam
- Halmi
- Mudang

Common nouns may be translated when the English term adequately conveys the meaning, but the Korean term may still be retained when it is important to the performance tradition.

## 3. Performance Structure

**과장 (gwajang)** is rendered as **Scene** in the English script.

This is a functional translation for international readers. *Gwajang* refers to a distinct performance unit within the larger mask dance rather than a chapter of a written work.

**연희본** is rendered as **performance script** or **performance text**, depending on context.

**연희** is normally rendered as **performance**.

## 4. Characters and Social Roles

Do not flatten distinct Korean character types into the same English label.

For example, Sangjwa, Mokjung, and No-seung all have Buddhist associations, but they are distinct dramatic roles. They should not all simply become “Monk.”

Explanatory English descriptions are glosses, not replacements for character names.

Similarly, **Yangban** is normally retained rather than replaced throughout by “aristocrat” or “nobleman.” An explanatory gloss such as “member of the traditional elite” may be supplied for readers.

## 5. Religious and Ritual Language

Religious and ritual vocabulary must be translated according to its function in context.

Terms such as:

- byeoksa (벽사)
- gut (굿)
- mudang (무당)
- Buddhist titles and objects
- invocations of spirits, deities, kings, lords, or honored beings

must not be mapped mechanically to English religious vocabulary.

In particular, Korean honorific or supernatural expressions involving words such as **임금**, **대감**, **님**, or **신** must be interpreted from the surrounding ritual, social, and dramatic context before choosing words such as *king*, *lord*, *master*, *deity*, or *spirit*.

When uncertainty remains, preserve the Korean expression or add an explanatory note rather than forcing a false equivalence.

## 6. Music and Dance

**장단 (jangdan)** is retained as *jangdan* and explained as a rhythmic cycle/framework.

Named jangdan such as Taryeong, Gutgeori, Domburi, and Dol are retained in romanization.

**불림 (bullim)** is retained because it is a performative call connecting the actor and musicians, not merely spoken dialogue.

**춤사위 (chumsawi)** is retained where the named Korean dance concept matters. In explanatory prose it may be glossed as “dance movement” or “dance phrase.”

**잽이 (jaebi)** is retained when referring to the traditional musicians as participants in the dramatic exchange.

## 7. Costumes and Props

Culturally specific costume and prop names are normally retained and glossed:

- hansam (한삼) — long sleeve extensions
- gokkal (고깔) — conical ceremonial hat
- jangsam (장삼) — Buddhist robe
- gasa (가사) — Buddhist ceremonial vestment
- songnak (송낙) — monk’s hat
- baekpal yeomju (백팔염주) — 108-bead Buddhist rosary
- moktak (목탁) — wooden Buddhist percussion instrument

The English gloss should help the reader visualize or understand the object without replacing its Korean name where cultural specificity matters.

## 8. Dialogue

Dialogue should sound speakable rather than like literal academic prose.

Preserve, where possible:

- comic timing;
- repetition;
- boastful or exaggerated speech;
- social register;
- verbal sparring;
- rhythm and momentum.

Do not modernize the dialogue so aggressively that its historical or folk-performance character disappears.

Dialectal or archaic Korean should not automatically be converted into a specific English regional dialect. Such a choice could create cultural associations absent from the source.

## 9. Calls, Cries, and Non-Lexical Sounds

Calls, cries, exclamations, vocables, and rhythmic sounds should normally be preserved rather than deleted merely because they lack a straightforward lexical translation.

Where the sound itself is performatively important, romanization or a close phonetic representation is preferred.

If its function is known, a stage note may explain it.

## 10. Songs and Verse

Sung passages should be distinguished from ordinary dialogue.

The first priority is semantic and cultural accuracy. Rhyme or meter should not be invented at the cost of meaning.

Where repetition or rhythmic phrasing is important to performance, reproduce it as far as natural English permits.

## 11. Stage Directions

Stage directions should be clear enough for a reader to understand the physical action.

They should be visually distinguished from dialogue, normally with italics and/or parentheses in Markdown.

Names of Korean dance movements, musical patterns, costumes, and props should remain consistent with the guide.

Do not silently omit repeated movements simply because they seem redundant in written English; repetition may be structurally important in performance.

## 12. Scene Files

Each Scene file should contain:

1. the Korean and English Scene title;
2. a concise list of characters appearing in that Scene;
3. short one-line glosses where useful for immediate reading;
4. the translated performance script.

Longer character and cultural explanations belong in `guide.md`, avoiding unnecessary duplication.

## 13. Source Use

The principal source text is:

**[ 8 ] 은율탈춤의 연희본.pdf**

The accompanying academic materials [1]–[7] are used to clarify performance structure, character identity, masks, costumes, props, music, dance, and cultural or ritual meaning.

**[ 9 ] 영문요약.pdf** may be consulted as evidence of prior English terminology, but its wording is not automatically adopted. Terminology is chosen for accuracy, clarity, consistency, and preservation of Korean cultural distinctions.

## 14. Working Rule for Ambiguity

When a line cannot be translated confidently from the script alone:

1. check the surrounding dialogue and stage directions;
2. consult the relevant supporting academic material;
3. compare terminology already established in `guide.md` and this document;
4. preserve ambiguity where the Korean itself is ambiguous;
5. record any consequential translation decision here.

The translation should not conceal uncertainty by inventing a precise English meaning unsupported by the source.


## 15. Completeness and Fidelity Standard

The final translation is intended to be a **complete translation of the performance text**, not an abridged adaptation.

Accordingly:

- every spoken line in the source should have a corresponding English rendering;
- every song stanza, refrain, *bullim*, cry, and vocable should be represented;
- stage directions should not be summarized when the omitted detail affects movement, spacing, timing, costume, props, music, or interaction;
- long catalogues of place names, literary allusions, wordplay, and enumerations should not be replaced by editorial summaries merely for readability;
- where literal wordplay cannot survive in English, retain the translated line and add a concise note explaining the Korean mechanism;
- source annotations that materially change the interpretation of a line should be carried into a translation note;
- uncertain or corrupt transmitted wording should be marked as such rather than silently emended.

A scene containing phrases such as “the speech continues” or “a long chain of wordplay follows” is therefore considered a **working draft**, not a completed translation, until the corresponding source passage has been translated in full.

## 16. Source-Fidelity Review Status

Each scene should be treated as passing through three states:

1. **Draft** — overall dramatic meaning and structure translated.
2. **Source-aligned** — checked line by line against [8], with omissions and editorial compression removed.
3. **Reviewed** — terminology and cultural interpretation cross-checked against [1]–[7] and the project guide.

Only a source-aligned and reviewed scene should be described as final.
